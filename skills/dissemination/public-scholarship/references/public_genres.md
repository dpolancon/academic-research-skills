# Public Scholarship Genres: Architecture and Workflow

Public-facing writing is not a single rigid template. Different public outlets, reading contexts, and rhetorical purposes require distinct structural designs.

---

## 1. Genre Archetypes

### 1.1 Contemporary Op-Ed (800–1,200 words)
- **Primary Audience:** General newspaper readers, policy observers, engaged citizens.
- **Urgency:** Immediate connection to breaking news, pending legislation, or current economic events.
- **Architecture:**
  1. *The Hook (1 paragraph):* Direct engagement with the contemporary event or debate.
  2. *The Core Thesis (1 sentence):* Clear, declarative statement of the argument up front.
  3. *The Mechanism (2–3 paragraphs):* Focus on 1–2 primary structural drivers explaining why common wisdom is mistaken.
  4. *The Counter-Argument (1 paragraph):* Acknowledge and dismantle the orthodox or opposing explanation.
  5. *The Call / Takeaway (1 paragraph):* Concise policy implication or political lesson.

### 1.2 Explanatory Public Essay (1,500–2,500 words)
- **Primary Audience:** Students, trade unionists, educators, movement organizers, analytical journalists.
- **Urgency:** Medium-term; educational illumination of enduring economic puzzles.
- **Architecture:**
  1. *The Phenomenon (1–2 paragraphs):* Ground the puzzle in lived experience or concrete economic anomalies.
  2. *The Analytical Dilemma (2–3 paragraphs):* Expose why conventional or mainstream frameworks fail to account for the phenomenon.
  3. *The Institutional Machinery (4–6 paragraphs):* Step-by-step exposition of the political-economic mechanism (e.g., pricing power, credit cycles, external vulnerability).
  4. *Empirical Grounding (3–4 paragraphs):* Concrete data, historical evidence, or illustrative comparisons.
  5. *Strategic Outlook (2 paragraphs):* Structural stakes and future political horizons.

### 1.3 Historical Perspective (1,500–3,000 words)
- **Primary Audience:** Historical readers, political researchers, activists reflecting on strategic precedents.
- **Urgency:** Reflective; connecting historical episodes to current structural dilemmas.
- **Architecture:**
  1. *The Historical Question (1–2 paragraphs):* Introduce the historical event, crisis, or reform sequence.
  2. *Demystifying the Official Myth (2–3 paragraphs):* Contrast orthodox historical narratives with material records.
  3. *Reconstructing the Contradiction (4–8 paragraphs):* Trace the sequence of class pressures, balance-of-payments stresses, or institutional bottlenecks using primary and secondary evidence.
  4. *The Living Precedent (2–3 paragraphs):* Translate historical findings into structural warnings or insights for contemporary economic governance.

### 1.4 Policy Brief / Memorandum (1,200–2,000 words)
- **Primary Audience:** Legislative staff, union leadership, policy advocates, government officials.
- **Urgency:** High; focused on institutional decisions, regulatory interventions, or bargaining strategies.
- **Architecture:**
  1. *Executive Summary / BLUF (1 paragraph):* Problem, evidence finding, and core recommendation.
  2. *Context & Problem Statement (2–3 paragraphs):* The market failure, regulatory gap, or distributive imbalance.
  3. *Assessment of Alternatives (3–5 paragraphs):* Concrete evaluation of policy options and institutional trade-offs.
  4. *Implementation Risks & Safeguards (2–3 paragraphs):* Fiscal, administrative, and legal considerations.
  5. *Recommendations & Roadmap (1–2 paragraphs):* Phased action points.

## 2. Crafting Titles Across Genres

- **Assertive Thesis Titles (Jacobin, Monthly Review):**  
  Declarative sentences that state the conclusion clearly:  
  *Example:* *"Contra el mito de que el gasto social produce inflación"*
- **Question Titles (Magazines, Op-eds):**  
  Engaging questions that challenge conventional wisdom:  
  *Example:* *"Why Central Banks Kept Raising Rates When Prices Were Already Falling"*
- **Two-Part Colon Titles (New York Times, Boston Review):**  
  Broad topic followed by a specific analytical focus:  
  *Example:* *"The Cost of Living: How Corporate Markups Distort Inflation Politics"*
- **Policy Memorandum Titles:**  
  Direct, institutional, and functional:  
  *Example:* *"Policy Brief: Mitigating Pass-Through Inflation in Public Utility Tariffs"*
