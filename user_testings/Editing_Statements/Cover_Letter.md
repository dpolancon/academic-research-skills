Dear Members of the Selection Committee,

Economic history often turns on categories that look settled only because their construction has disappeared from view. My research begins from that problem. I study how measures such as productive capacity, profitability, functional distribution, and national income are built, contested, and used to interpret capitalist development. I am applying for the LSE Fellowship in Economic History because the post calls for the combination that has shaped my doctoral training: historical political economy, quantitative methods, research-led teaching, and close work with students as they learn to move between evidence, explanation, and argument.

I am a PhD candidate in Economics at the University of Massachusetts Amherst, with expected completion in Summer 2026. My dissertation, *Capacity Utilization and Accumulation Regimes: Essays in the Political Economy of Growth, Distribution, and Core–Periphery Dynamics*, studies capacity utilization and accumulation as central historical subjects of inquiry in political economy. Across the United States and Chile, I reconstruct how productive capacity, profitability, national-income categories, and external constraints have been measured, contested, and embedded in institutions. Methodologically, I integrate applied time-series econometrics and historical data reconstruction with national-accounting interpretation and comparative-historical analysis. The dissertation's central concern is direct: measures often treated as neutral technical baselines are shaped by distributional conflict, labor relations, state institutions, and core–periphery hierarchy.

My current projects extend this focus by investigating how measurement infrastructures shape macroeconomic policy and historical narratives. My article, _“The Ideological Embeddedness of Macroeconomic Indexes: Capacity Utilization in the Rise and Fall of American Keynesianism,”_ currently submitted for review, reconstructs the institutional history of U.S. capacity-utilization statistics with attention to American Keynesianism, Fordist growth, and the politics of statistical categories. A second project, _“Capital-Stock Harmonization for Chile, 1900–2024: A Stock–Flow Consistent Historical Data Infrastructure,”_ builds harmonized long-run capital-stock series by linking a canonical 1900–1994 benchmark with a forward extension through the Central Bank of Chile (BCCh) to 2024. This work is not only technical; it asks what kind of historical account becomes possible when the measurement of accumulation is rebuilt from the ground up rather than inherited as a ready-made series.

Because statistical categories are constructed by historical actors, my quantitative work is coupled with qualitative and archival methods. A recent co-authored working paper, _“Intellectual Trajectory Mapping: A Critical-Realist Method for Fractured Oral Histories,”_ develops a method for analysing interrupted oral-history corpora and applies it to economists and political figures connected to Chile’s Unidad Popular. That project grew from a practical problem: testimony shaped by rupture, memory, access constraints, and political silence cannot be treated simply as incomplete evidence. It has to be read as a structured historical object. This critical-realist approach recovers the intellectual and political conflicts behind economic policy during periods of crisis, ensuring that quantitative data is not divorced from the historical struggles that produced it.

Teaching has been central to my development as a scholar rather than an activity added around research. Since 2009, I have designed and taught courses in economic history, political economy, and quantitative methods across diverse institutional contexts in the United States and Chile—including UMass Amherst, Wesleyan University, Amherst College, and the Universidad de Chile. My courses ask students to connect concepts to evidence: to read data historically, to identify the assumptions inside economic categories, and to write arguments that can survive contact with institutions, cases, and measurement problems.

Between 2021 and 2025, I also designed and delivered ten online economics courses through UMass Amherst and its U+ program, including Economic Development and Writing in Economics. Managing these courses taught me a pedagogical discipline that is easy to underestimate: structuring virtual learning environments to keep students engaged, scaffolding assignments in stages, and maintaining active communication across uneven schedules and preparation levels.

In advising, my focus is on helping students translate broad interests into researchable questions and empirical strategies. Teaching graduate-level public policy and research design has allowed me to supervise student projects spanning comparative institutional analysis, tax reform, and developmental policy. My advising practice is built around structured milestones, early question definition, and feedback that sharpens the analytical core of a project rather than merely correcting its surface. I am fully prepared to bring this approach to undergraduate and MSc dissertation supervision at LSE.

At LSE, I am eager to teach across the economic history curriculum, particularly in quantitative methods, macroeconomic history, and Latin American development. I am especially prepared to guide students who are learning to bridge the gap between historical narrative and quantitative evidence—whether by interpreting national accounts, constructing long-run series, or turning broad historical questions into researchable empirical designs. I also look forward to contributing fully to the collegiate life of the department, whether through course coordination, Virtual Learning Environment (VLE) management, or participating in the department’s research seminars and student workshops.

The LSE Fellowship is uniquely compelling because it treats research development and teaching excellence as mutually reinforcing. My research investigates how economic categories are constructed historically; my teaching trains students to examine how those categories organize evidence and shape public understanding. I would be delighted to bring this combination of historical inquiry and quantitative rigor to the Department of Economic History at LSE.
