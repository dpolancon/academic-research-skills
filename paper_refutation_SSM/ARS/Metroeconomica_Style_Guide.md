# Deconstructing the 'Metroeconomica' Manuscript: An LLM-Driven Template for Post-Keynesian Mathematical Refutations

This report provides a comprehensive analysis of the structural, stylistic, and technical requirements for submitting an analytical paper featuring formal mathematical proofs to the journal *Metroeconomica*. The research synthesizes information from official publisher guidelines, recent publications within the journal, and established academic writing conventions to construct a detailed style guide. This guide is designed to function as a rulebook for an LLM drafting agent, ensuring that the output manuscript aligns precisely with the expectations of the journal's editorial board and readership. The focus is on papers engaging with post-Keynesian and Sraffian debates, particularly those involving formal modeling and theoretical refutation.

## Structural Norms and Content Balance in Analytical Economics

*Metroeconomica* positions itself as a global forum for debate in analytical economics, addressing core issues such as social institutions, technical change, income distribution, and methodological innovations [[9](https://scholar9.com/journal/metroeconomica-30014), [10](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/journal/1467999x)]. Its identity as a journal of analytical economics implies a strong preference for rigorously formalized models and clear, logical argumentation, making it a suitable venue for papers centered on mathematical proofs [[62](https://www.wiley.com/en-us/Metroeconomica-p-1467999X)]. For a short paper or "Note," which is highly relevant to the user's goal, the structural norms are stringent and demand conciseness without sacrificing analytical depth [[11](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/meca.12407), [55](https://tektonika.online/index.php/home/manuscript_guidelines)]. Successful submissions typically follow a clear, deductive flow that efficiently communicates a specific, well-contained contribution. The ideal structure for a short note refuting the Sraffian Supermultiplier would distill the broader narrative of a full-length article into its most essential components, focusing relentlessly on the core proof. The standard flow observed in recent analytical contributions to the journal is Introduction -> Baseline Model/Formal Setup -> Propositions and Proofs -> Conclusion [[1](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/page/journal/1467999x/homepage/forauthors.html)]. This structure ensures that the reader is first oriented within the existing literature, then presented with the precise assumptions of the new model, followed by the rigorous demonstration of the result, and finally, the implications of that result.

The Introduction is critical for establishing context and justifying the paper's novelty. In the case of a refutation, it must frame the contribution not as a polemical attack but as a necessary clarification of a theoretical mechanism. It should briefly introduce the Sraffian Supermultiplier (SSM) and its central claim about long-run convergence, citing key sources like Serrano (1995) and Freitas & Serrano (2015) [[18](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/meca.12457), [76](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/meca.12377)]. The introduction of the narrower paper—without endogenous technical change—must explicitly state its purpose: to demonstrate that the fundamental assumption of long-run equilibrium in the baseline SSM fails even under its own simplest, fixed-coefficient setup when confronted with unbalanced growth [[12](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/meca.12400)]. This positioning is strategically powerful because it argues against a knife-edge condition rather than an overly complex extension of the model. The literature review within the introduction should be brief but targeted, referencing recent discussions of the SSM's stability and convergence properties, particularly works by authors frequently published in *Metroeconomica*, such as Michalis Nikiforos, who has written extensively on capacity utilization and demand-led growth [[101](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/meca.12476), [102](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/manc.12423), [103](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/joes.12614)]. By directly engaging with these contemporary debates, the paper situates itself at the forefront of the journal's thematic interests.

Following the introduction, the Baseline Model section must present the formal setup with maximum clarity and precision. For the proposed refutation, this involves defining the variables of the Sraffian Supermultiplier model, including output, autonomous demand, and capacity, while explicitly assuming a fixed, exogenous capital-output ratio ($\nu$) [[75](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/meca.70011)]. This section should contain the core dynamic equations governing the system, such as the equation for investment induced by demand and utilization, and the definition of the capacity utilization rate. The presentation here should be purely mathematical, using standard notation consistent with the target literature. The goal is to provide the reader with a complete and unambiguous description of the model being analyzed before any claims about its behavior are made. This formal setup serves as the foundation upon which the subsequent proof rests.

The heart of the paper, and the section that demands the greatest concentration of effort, is the one containing the Propositions and Proofs. This is where the paper's main contribution is delivered. The structure here is often dictated by the nature of the result itself. A common format in *Metroeconomica* is to state a proposition clearly and then immediately follow it with its proof [[37](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/jpet.70078), [38](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/meca.12382)]. The balance between economic intuition and formal mathematics in this section is delicate. While the primary vehicle of argumentation is the mathematical derivation, the text preceding and following the equations should provide essential economic interpretation. For instance, before presenting the proof of non-convergence, the text should explain why the assumption of unbalanced growth ($\theta \neq 1$) is economically meaningful and how the fixed coefficient assumption isolates a specific type of instability [[73](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/meca.12490)]. The mathematical steps themselves should be presented logically and sequentially, showing how the initial conditions lead to the final conclusion of Harrodian instability [[71](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/meca.12230)]. The language must remain formal and precise, avoiding casual or speculative phrasing. The entire section should be self-contained, allowing a technically proficient reader to verify the result without referring back to earlier parts of the paper.

Finally, the Conclusion summarizes the findings and their significance for the field. It should reiterate the main result—that the baseline SSM lacks a long-run steady state under unbalanced growth—and connect this finding back to the broader post-Keynesian and Sraffian debates. It could discuss the implications for theories of demand-led growth, the stability of supermultiplier models, or the general validity of Harrodian instability arguments [[74](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/meca.12109)]. This section is also where the paper's limitations are acknowledged; for example, it might note that the analysis assumes fixed coefficients and does not explore the effects of endogenous technical change, thereby inviting future research. The tone should be confident yet measured, reflecting the strength of the mathematical proof without overstating its generality beyond what was demonstrated. This concise, logical structure—from introduction to conclusion—is characteristic of high-quality analytical work in *Metroeconomica* and is the optimal framework for a short paper aiming to make a sharp, focused theoretical contribution.

## Mathematical Formatting Standards and Proof Presentation

The presentation of formal mathematics is a hallmark of analytical economics journals, and *Metroeconomica* is no exception. Adherence to specific standards for formatting propositions, theorems, proofs, and dynamic equations is crucial for conveying rigor and ensuring clarity. Analysis of recent publications reveals a consistent reliance on standard LaTeX packages and environments, particularly `amsthm`, which provides structured ways to typeset mathematical content [[122](https://authors.wiley.com/asset/Wiley-Journals-Style-Manual.pdf)]. A "Style and Presentation Guide" for an LLM agent must codify these conventions into explicit instructions to guarantee the final manuscript meets the journal's typographical expectations. Failure to do so can lead to desk rejection based on poor presentation, regardless of the paper's intellectual merit.

The most prominent feature of mathematical presentation in *Metroeconomica* papers is the use of theorem-like environments. Authors routinely employ `\begin{proposition}`, `\begin{theorem}`, and `\begin{proof}` environments to delineate different components of their arguments [[96](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/meca.12337), [97](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/meca.12517), [98](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/meca.12511)]. These environments not only add visual structure but also semantically categorize the content, signaling to the reader what kind of statement is being made. For instance, a key result demonstrating the failure of long-run convergence in the Sraffian Supermultiplier under unbalanced growth would be best presented as a proposition. The proposition should be stated formally, often with a descriptive title enclosed in square brackets, such as `\begin{proposition}[Harrodian Instability in Unbalanced Growth]`. This makes the content easily scannable and referenceable throughout the paper. The use of these environments is a direct reflection of the journal's commitment to formal logic and deductive reasoning.

The placement of proofs is another critical aspect of mathematical formatting. In the vast majority of cases, proofs are placed immediately following the statement of the proposition they support [[37](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/jpet.70078), [38](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/meca.12382), [77](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/meca.12276)]. This convention enhances readability by keeping the claim and its justification co-located, preventing the reader from having to flip pages to find the verification of a previous assertion. It is rare for the core proof of a central result to be relegated to an appendix, though appendices are used for auxiliary results, lengthy derivations, or supplementary data [[40](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/pdf/10.1111/meca.12381), [43](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/meca.12381)]. The environment itself is typically named `\begin{proof}`. Often, the word "Proof." is set on its own line, flush left, before the mathematical derivation begins, serving as a clear demarcation [[77](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/meca.12276)]. Some papers may conclude the proof with a small square symbol ($\blacksquare$) or Q.E.D. to signal its completion, although this is less common in economics than in pure mathematics. The LLM drafting agent must be instructed to always place the `\begin{proof}` environment directly after the corresponding `\begin{proposition}` environment.

Dynamic equations and steady-state derivations are presented as standalone, numbered entities to facilitate discussion and citation within the text. The `equation` or `align` environments from the `amsmath` package are the standard tools for this purpose [[105](https://www.latexstudio.net/uploads/20250805/c2f84ea275dac7c0a85bf9cc461a7ed4.pdf)]. For example, the dynamic equation for the capacity utilization rate, $\dot{\mu}/\mu = f(\cdot)$, would be set apart from the surrounding prose. When deriving a steady state, authors show each algebraic step clearly, often with explanatory text in between lines to describe the manipulation being performed (e.g., "Setting $\dot{\mu}/\mu = 0$ and solving for the utilization rate..."). This step-by-step exposition is vital for maintaining transparency and allowing peer reviewers and readers to follow the logic. The `align` environment is particularly useful for multi-line derivations, as it allows for alignment at specific points, such as equality signs, improving readability immensely. The LLM agent should be guided to use these environments liberally for all significant equations and derivations.

To operationalize these standards for an LLM, the style guide should include concrete LaTeX code snippets. Below is a template illustrating the correct formatting for a key proposition and its proof, tailored to the user's research goal.

```latex
% Ensure required packages are included in the preamble
\usepackage{amsmath}
\usepackage{amsthm}

% Define custom theorem/proof styles if needed, or use defaults
\newtheorem{proposition}{Proposition}

\section{The Proof of Long-Run Instability}
% ... text introducing the proposition ...

\begin{proposition}[Non-Existence of Steady State under Unbalanced Growth]
Consider the baseline Sraffian Supermultiplier model with a fixed capital-output ratio $\nu$. If the economy experiences unbalanced growth such that the growth rate of autonomous demand components differs across sectors ($\theta \neq 1$), then the system has no interior steady state for the capacity utilization rate.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let $Y$ denote output, $A$ denote autonomous demand, and $K$ denote capital stock. The dynamics of capacity utilization, $\mu = Y/K$, are given by the following equation:
\begin{equation}
\frac{\dot{\mu}}{\mu} = g_A - g_K + \frac{\dot{v}}{v} - \frac{\dot{\mu}}{\mu} \text{ (example form)}
\end{equation}
where $g_A$ is the growth rate of autonomous demand and $g_K$ is the growth rate of capital. Under the assumption of a fixed capital-output ratio, $\dot{v}/v = 0$. In a balanced growth path, $g_A = g_K = g^*$, leading to $\dot{\mu}/\mu = 0$ and thus a steady state. However, under unbalanced growth with $\theta \neq 1$, the weighted average growth rate of autonomous demand cannot equal the constant growth rate of capacity, which grows at the rate of the largest component of $A$. This implies $\dot{\mu}/\mu \neq 0$ in the long run, and therefore, the utilization rate diverges. This demonstrates Harrodian instability and proves the proposition.
\end{proof}
```

## Tone, Rhetoric, and Literature Engagement

The rhetorical approach taken in a paper submitted to *Metroeconomica* is as important as its mathematical content, especially when the paper's objective is to present a refutation. Heterodox economics journals value rigorous debate and critical engagement, but they expect this to be conducted with intellectual precision and respect for the subject matter [[94](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/meca.12466)]. A paper titled "A Refutation to the Sraffian Super Multiplier" risks being perceived as polemical and may be rejected on stylistic grounds alone [[41](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/dech.70031)]. Therefore, the tone must be carefully calibrated to position the contribution as a valuable clarification of a theoretical mechanism rather than a personal critique of its originators. The literature engagement must be formal, precise, and deeply contextualized within the ongoing scholarly conversation.

The most effective rhetorical strategy is to frame the paper's contribution as a demonstration of a logical consequence or a limitation of the baseline model, rather than a wholesale rejection of the entire theoretical project. Instead of claiming to have "refuted" the SSM, the language should focus on the conditions under which its central claim—long-run convergence—holds. A more suitable framing would be to argue that the paper shows the long-run convergence of the SSM requires a "knife-edge" assumption of balanced growth that is generically violated in a multi-component economy [[12](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/meca.12400)]. This approach shifts the debate from one of victory or defeat to one of clarifying the robustness and applicability of the model's core mechanisms. The language should consistently refer to the SSM as a "model," a "theory," or a "framework" developed by specific authors (e.g., Serrano, Freitas), which maintains a professional distance and focuses the critique on the formalism itself. The goal is to contribute to the collective understanding of demand-led growth theory by highlighting a source of instability that may have been previously overlooked or underemphasized [[74](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/meca.12109)].

Engaging with the literature requires a similar level of nuance. The paper must situate its argument within the specific debates that have already taken place in *Metroeconomica* and related journals. This involves citing the foundational papers on the SSM, such as Freitas and Serrano (2015), and acknowledging the subsequent literature that has explored its properties [[18](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/meca.12457)]. Crucially, it should also engage with recent contributions from the same authors and their colleagues that discuss related issues like Harrodian instability, the stability of supermultipliers, and the convergence of capacity utilization rates [[101](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/meca.12476), [102](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/manc.12423), [131](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/meca.12016)]. For example, the paper could state: "While recent work has explored the taming of Harrodian instability in neo-Kaleckian models, this note demonstrates that the core logic of the Sraffian Supermultiplier itself is fundamentally unstable under more general conditions of unbalanced growth" [[74](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/meca.12109)]. This type of engagement shows deep familiarity with the field and frames the new contribution as part of an ongoing scholarly dialogue. It avoids creating a false dichotomy and instead builds upon the existing body of knowledge.

When presenting the mathematical refutation, the tone must remain dispassionate and objective. The focus should be on the logical implications of the model's equations. For example, the proof should be presented as a sequence of deductions: "Given the assumptions of the model, we derive the following dynamic equation. From this equation, it follows that..." rather than "We prove that the model is wrong because...". This objective tone is essential for maintaining credibility. The justification for the paper's specific assumptions is also a key part of the rhetorical strategy. As the user correctly identified, narrowing the scope to a model with a fixed capital-output ratio ($\nu$) is a strategic advantage [[12](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/meca.12400)]. This choice must be justified in the text, arguing that by proving the baseline model is flawed, the paper delivers a more powerful critique than one that relies on more complex extensions involving endogenous technical change. The justification should read: "To isolate the core mechanism, we analyze the model under its most basic assumptions, demonstrating that even in this simple setting, the long-run closure fails under unbalanced growth."

In summary, the ideal rhetorical stance for the paper is that of a meticulous analyst uncovering a hidden flaw in a widely cited model. The tone should be formal, the language precise, and the engagement with the literature deep and respectful. The paper's contribution is not to dismiss the SSM, but to refine our understanding of its domain of applicability by proving that its celebrated long-run convergence property is contingent on implausible, knife-edge conditions. This nuanced approach is far more likely to be well-received by the editors and reviewers of a premier analytical journal like *Metroeconomica*.

## Technical Submission Requirements and Journal-Specific Rules

Beyond content and style, adherence to the technical submission requirements set forth by the publisher, Wiley, and the journal itself is a non-negotiable prerequisite for consideration. Desk rejection due to formatting errors is a common hurdle, making it essential to consult the official author guidelines meticulously [[7](https://authors.wiley.com/author-compliance-tool.html), [8](https://authors.wiley.com/author-resources/Journal-Authors/submission-peer-review/index.html)]. For an LLM drafting agent, these requirements must be translated into strict, executable instructions. The key areas of concern are the manuscript preparation format (specifically LaTeX), the bibliography and citation style, and the specific rules for short papers or notes.

Wiley provides official LaTeX templates for its journals to ensure consistency in appearance across publications [[64](https://scispace.com/formats/wiley/metroeconomica/760a2b30ecd5f6c0aaa1ac15cc720236), [65](https://authors.wiley.com/author-resources/Journal-Authors/Prepare/latex-template.html)]. Using one of these templates is the recommended way to prepare a manuscript, as it pre-configures fonts, margins, headings, and other layout elements according to the journal's specifications [[107](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SZuSYMn6sjI)]. The user should download the specific LaTeX template for *Metroeconomica* from the journal's homepage or the Wiley Author Gateway [[66](https://www.overleaf.com/latex/templates/wiley-journal-template/nnjcnfyhzcqp), [105](https://www.latexstudio.net/uploads/20250805/c2f84ea275dac7c0a85bf9cc461a7ed4.pdf)]. The LLM agent should be instructed to generate the entire manuscript using this template as its base. This guarantees that the final PDF will meet the publisher's house style. Key parameters to verify from the template include font size (typically 10pt or 12pt), line spacing (at least 1.5 is common), and margin sizes [[50](https://www.econometricsociety.org/publications/econometrica/information-authors)]. The abstract should be a concise summary of the paper's purpose, methods, and findings, typically around 150 words or less [[48](https://www.feministeconomics.org/for-authors/style-guidelines/), [54](https://publications.copernicus.org/for_authors/manuscript_preparation.html)].

The bibliography style is another critical technical detail. While many economics journals use numbered citation styles, *Metroeconomica* appears to favor the Harvard (Author-Date) style, which is common in the social sciences [[46](https://tex.stackexchange.com/questions/146159/how-do-i-make-my-bibliography-according-to-harvard-style-author-year-alphabeti), [51](https://libguides.cam.ac.uk/economics/referencing)]. This style uses parenthetical citations in the text (e.g., `(Freitas & Serrano, 2015)`) and arranges the full references in an alphabetized list at the end of the paper [[110](https://www.wiley.com/en-us/publish/book/steps/prepare-manuscript/house-style/)]. In a LaTeX workflow, this is typically managed using BibTeX [[135](https://library-guides.imperial.ac.uk/referencing-with-bibtex/bibtex-harvard-style)]. The LLM agent should be instructed to generate a `.bib` file containing all the necessary references and to use the appropriate bibliography style (.bst file) that corresponds to the Harvard style. Some university library guides confirm that Harvard style is expected for the journal [[47](https://wsu-ac.libguides.com/c.php?g=1231339&p=9011256), [53](https://www.citethisforme.com/citation-generator/harvard/harvard-leeds-metropolitan-university)]. It is important to distinguish between using BibTeX with the `plainnat` style versus the `natbib` package, which offers more control over citation commands [[137](https://tex.stackexchange.com/questions/197535/referencing-not-bibliography-in-harvard-style-using-write-latex)]. The official template or guidelines will specify the correct method. The agent should produce clean, properly formatted BibTeX entries for all cited works, including the foundational papers on the SSM and relevant contributions from Nikiforos, Serrano, and Freitas.

Finally, the paper must comply with any specific rules for short papers or "Notes" that *Metroeconomica* may have. The journal does publish Notes, which are described as short papers on novel ideas and are typically limited to around 3,500 words, excluding figures, tables, and references [[55](https://tektonika.online/index.php/home/manuscript_guidelines)]. While the provided search results do not contain the exact word count limit from the current author guidelines, this figure is representative of such sections in other major journals [[55](https://tektonika.online/index.php/home/manuscript_guidelines)]. The user should verify the precise word limit on the official *Metroeconomica* author guidelines page [[1](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/page/journal/1467999x/homepage/forauthors.html)]. The LLM agent must be constrained to produce a manuscript that falls within this limit. Other potential journal-specific rules could relate to the inclusion of an acknowledgments section, funding disclosures, or ethical compliance statements. These details should also be checked on the official guidelines and incorporated into the manuscript accordingly [[4](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/page/journal/1467999x/homepage/fundedaccess.html), [127](https://authors.wiley.com/author-resources/Journal-Authors/open-access/affiliation-policies-payments/institutional-funder-payments.html)]. By treating these technical requirements as hard constraints in the LLM's prompt, the resulting manuscript will be not only intellectually sound and stylistically appropriate but also technically compliant, maximizing its chances of progressing beyond the initial administrative screening stage.
