# Memo — Impacts of AI on My Doctoral Writing Process

## Purpose

This memo translates a recent review on AI and academic writing into a diagnosis of my own writing process under doctoral deadlines. The goal is not to moralize AI use, but to clarify where it strengthens my workflow, where it threatens my development, and what guardrails are needed to preserve my voice, conceptual control, and doctoral standards. The source review is useful precisely because it identifies a double movement: AI improves efficiency, fluency, and revision support, but can also weaken independent writing, critical thinking, and originality when overused. :contentReference[oaicite:0]{index=0}

## General Assessment

The review’s central insight is highly relevant to my situation. AI tools are strongest when they reduce drafting friction, provide instant feedback, improve grammar and coherence, and support revision. They become risky when they begin to substitute for the cognitive work of argument construction, analytical judgment, and original phrasing. Since my process is not undergraduate coursework but accelerated doctoral production in Political Economy, the stakes are even higher: the danger is not only ethical misuse, but the erosion of the very capacities that doctoral writing is meant to demonstrate. :contentReference[oaicite:1]{index=1}

## My Concrete Situation

I am using AI under conditions of compression. The immediate problem is not whether I can write, but how to sustain a high rate of conceptual and textual production while preserving coherence across multiple layers of work: theory, literature, historical analysis, methodology, and style.

In that context, AI has become a productivity infrastructure. It helps me:

- reduce friction in drafting and revising,
- test formulations quickly,
- restructure sections faster,
- compare alternatives in tone and cadence,
- condense, reorganize, and smooth prose under deadline pressure.

These functions align closely with the benefits identified in the review: enhanced fluency, self-directed revision, faster feedback, and lower cognitive load in drafting. :contentReference[oaicite:2]{index=2}

## The Core Risk

The problem begins when AI stops operating as an assistant and starts acting as a substitute for intellectual labor.

The review repeatedly warns that over-reliance on AI can weaken critical thinking, originality, deep engagement with the material, and long-term writing development. In my case, that warning must be reformulated at a higher level: the deepest risk is not simply that AI helps too much, but that it starts doing the very work through which I discover what I think. :contentReference[oaicite:3]{index=3}

Writing at the doctoral level is not merely a channel for expressing finished ideas. It is itself a method of theorizing. Through writing, I test conceptual distinctions, clarify mechanisms, decide levels of abstraction, and discover where an argument actually stands. If AI performs too much of that work, it may produce cleaner prose while quietly reducing my control over the argument’s formation.

## Authorial Smoothing as a Specific Danger

A more specific risk in my case is what might be called **authorial smoothing**.

AI often produces prose that is grammatically polished, structurally orderly, and rhetorically balanced. But that same fluency can make writing sound generic, over-symmetrical, and drained of the productive tensions that belong to real scholarly voice. The review notes that AI struggles with nuance, context, originality, and deeper rhetorical judgment. That matters especially in my field, where the force of writing often lies in conceptual precision, controlled abstraction, and historically grounded tension rather than in generalized polish. :contentReference[oaicite:4]{index=4}

This risk is already visible in my own writing process. My chapter has a strong conceptual architecture: a comparative-relational framework, nested analytical fields, intersecting temporalities, and Chile as a strategic condensation of wider contradictions. That architecture is authored and distinctive. But some prose segments tend toward dense stacks of abstraction that sound coherent while becoming slightly over-smoothed. In those moments, AI improves flow at the price of sharpness. :contentReference[oaicite:5]{index=5}

## Positive Impacts on My Process

When used under control, AI has several positive effects on my doctoral workflow.

First, it expands throughput. It lets me process more material, faster, without collapsing under the weight of revision.

Second, it improves editorial responsiveness. I can test alternative phrasings, cadences, and structures quickly, which is useful when refining my own voice rather than generating a new one.

Third, it supports architectural editing. It is especially effective for compression, section transitions, local restructuring, and identifying where prose becomes repetitive, diffuse, or too ceremonially academic.

Fourth, it can help preserve momentum. Under deadline pressure, momentum is not a luxury; it is a condition of completion. AI helps prevent stalling at the level of formulation.

These are real gains, and I should treat them as such rather than denying them. The review clearly supports the idea that AI can aid revision, efficiency, and self-directed learning when integrated carefully. :contentReference[oaicite:6]{index=6}

## Negative Impacts on My Process

The negative impacts are more subtle, and therefore more dangerous.

First, AI can tempt me to outsource argument formation rather than prose revision.

Second, it can replace friction with fluency too early. But friction is often where concepts become clear.

Third, it can produce false adequacy: paragraphs that look finished because they are smooth, even when they are still conceptually underdeveloped.

Fourth, it can standardize tone. This is especially risky in Political Economy, where voice is not ornament but part of how one stages a conceptual intervention.

Fifth, it can reduce my tolerance for slow thinking. If every blockage is solved immediately by generated language, I may lose the patience required for genuine theoretical work.

These dangers are all consistent with the review’s broader concern that AI can weaken independent reasoning, critical thinking, and originality when it becomes a crutch rather than a tool. :contentReference[oaicite:7]{index=7}

## Strategic Distinction: Outer Layers vs Inner Layers

The key operational distinction for my process is this:

AI is most useful on the **outer layers** of writing and most dangerous on the **inner layers**.

### Outer layers — legitimate AI support
- grammar and syntax cleanup,
- cadence testing,
- paragraph compression,
- transition repair,
- restructuring of sections,
- comparative rewriting of already-authored claims,
- editing for concision and readability.

### Inner layers — zones of strict authorial control
- formulation of the research problem,
- conceptual distinctions,
- theoretical intervention,
- mechanism specification,
- interpretation of evidence,
- historiographical judgment,
- final argumentative stakes,
- voice formation.

This distinction is the practical center of my AI discipline.

## Guardrails for My Workflow

To preserve doctoral integrity in the substantive sense, not merely the procedural one, I need the following rules.

### 1. AI cannot decide what I think
It may help me reformulate an argument, but it cannot determine the argument’s conceptual stakes.

### 2. AI must enter after the conceptual move is made
I should first decide the claim, distinction, or mechanism. Only then should AI help with phrasing, compression, or restructuring.

### 3. Drafting and theorizing are not the same task
I may use AI to accelerate drafting, but not to replace the slower work of theorization.

### 4. Smooth prose is not proof of strong writing
Any paragraph that sounds polished must still be checked for conceptual precision, authorial specificity, and argumentative necessity.

### 5. My voice must remain asymmetrical
If the prose becomes too uniformly balanced, neutral, or tidy, it is likely drifting away from my actual voice.

### 6. AI outputs must be treated as proposals, not solutions
Every output should be evaluated, cut, reshaped, or rejected according to my own standards.

## Operational Protocol

A productive sequence for my writing process is the following:

1. Define the paragraph or section’s job.
2. State the conceptual move in rough, author-led language.
3. Write or sketch the claim before asking for stylistic help.
4. Use AI to test alternatives, not to replace thinking.
5. Revise the output against my own criteria: precision, force, cadence, and voice.
6. Remove any sentence that sounds generic, interchangeable, or theatrically academic.

This is how AI can remain subordinated to authorial sovereignty rather than displacing it.

## Final Synthesis

AI is not simply helping me write faster. It is reorganizing the site of my labor.

The older difficulty was producing enough text. The current difficulty is ensuring that accelerated text production does not displace conceptual production.

Used correctly, AI strengthens my workflow by expanding throughput, supporting revision, and helping me preserve momentum. Used incorrectly, it weakens the very doctoral capacities I need to demonstrate: theoretical ownership, critical thinking, independent judgment, and a recognizable scholarly voice. The central strategic task is therefore not to reject AI, but to discipline it so that it serves authorship instead of diluting it. 

## Working Formula

Use AI to accelerate expression.  
Do not let it replace conceptual struggle.  
Use it to revise what I think.  
Do not let it think in my place.
